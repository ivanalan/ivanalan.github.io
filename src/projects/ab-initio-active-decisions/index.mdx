## Outcome

1. Ready to ship in 9 months against a 12-month deadline.
2. Ran a real-world trial with a major gaming brand, using Decision Flows in their store and pricing systems.
3. Replaced a tech-first architecture plan with a prioritised, user-centred roadmap.

## Context: turning real time data into automated actions

Active>360 lets enterprise marketers track real-time customer data and define custom metrics without IT. For example: track cellular data use over 30 days, by texting, streaming, and gaming.

## The problem: a 1-year MVP with no PRD

We had 12 months to take Decision Flows from planning to implementation. The only plan was a 15+ page software architecture doc: services and systems, but no problem framing, personas, user flows, IA, UI, or timelines.

I was the sole designer on a team of ~20 developers led by a senior engineer. No researchers, no direct end users, no use cases, no PM, no other designers. So, I effectively became the lead designer for Decision Flows.

## My role: lead planning, design, and implementation

While I was initially expected to design screens, I fought for UX from the beginning. So, I earned my place as a key decision maker and made most product calls like problem framing, IA, roadmap, MVP scope, stakeholder alignment, and interaction design. My engineering lead owned architecture, work delegation, and outreach to interested customers.

## From architecture doc to user-driven plan

I reverse-engineered user tasks from the architecture doc and checked them with proxy users (account managers). Then I ran a workshop with the full engineering team to agree feature sets and priorities. That became a UX north star alongside the technical plan, plus an initial IA and a year-long roadmap with UX and eng dependencies.

## Design approach: low fidelity on the hard parts

The company and domain were new to me, so I validated low-fi early with account managers. With a fixed deadline I spent design depth on the hardest surface: authoring automated logic as flow charts. I started that work early and ran recurring reviews on direction, interaction design, and technical constraints. Front-end engineers interpolated simpler screens.

## Two MVP calls: where to slow down and where to speed up

The engineering lead advocated for a mode where users could test an algorithm before publishing to production. However, I had talked with account managers who said customers already had workable ways to test, so I pushed back. This new testing mode would have delayed the MVP, so I reframed it as a later, product-wide monitoring launch.

Active>360’s navigation was confusing as it mirrored its backend where we had a top-level page per feature. Keeping this IA for Decision Flows would have made the site significantly hard to navigate, so I pushed to add in another piece of work for the MVP to redesign navigation.

## Impact: a real workflow with a major brand.

Before the funding cut, we trialled Decision Flows with a major gaming brand/console as part of their store recommendation and pricing systems. That was the closest thing we had to production proof: a real customer workflow, not an internal demo.

## What I’d do differently

This project changed how I operate: do more than “design screens,” even when that is the expectation. My work to turn this technical project into a user-driven product was valued by our team, even when it was an unexpected and new process.

I would also have protected low-fidelity exploration longer. Direction moved faster than it needed to, as I was worried about my roadmap, and a few more cheap iterations before locking flows would have helped.
