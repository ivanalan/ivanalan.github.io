## “When are you free?”

Every 4 days, my partner has a 24-hour overnight shift at the hospital. With such a physically and emotionally tiring job, those nights shape the rest of her week and month.

The catch is the pattern itself. A 4-day cycle doesn’t sit cleanly on a 7-day calendar. It isn’t as easy as saying “I always work Mondays and Thursdays,” and it’s hard to share with friends and family.

<ShiftCalendar />

So everyone asks the same question: “when are you free?”

## The job wasn’t just “track my shifts”

Creating a recurring schedule pattern for yourself only goes so far, and where most other apps stop. The real job was making “when are you free?” easily answerable without another back-and-forth.

So I built sharing around a single link: if someone already has Mi Guardia, it opens with the rotation pre-filled and ready to save; if they don’t, the same page still lets them export to Google Calendar, download an .ics, or grab an image.

<ShareEmbed />

## What other apps miss

Google Calendar and most shift trackers solve the narrow version: repeat this event every four days. The needs I kept running into went further:

- Counting down the shifts and days left in a brutal rotation
- Seeing when the one free weekend a month actually falls, so you can plan rest
- Overlaying a friend’s schedule against your own
- Getting the rotation out of your phone and into someone else’s

## I built the wrong MVP

In corporate software, an MVP usually means letting users create something and see it - sharing, commenting, and other collaboration can wait. I used that same habit here: first create a rotation, then visualize it in calendars and widgets.

The real MVP was the reverse: define a recurring rotation, then share or export it. Everything else was secondary. I shipped the share link late, after the app already felt “done.”

Looking back, I’d put sharing (and whatever it depends on) first. That gap taught me a lot about how PMs prioritize and how this can diverge from a UX or engineering instincts.

## Iterating over waiting

When I started, I planned a lot upfront: information architecture, requirements, edge cases, libraries. While the IA held strong across the whole SDLC, almost everything else changed. For example, the UI stack changed from React Native Paper → NativeWind, and I found better data visualizations only while building.

What mattered most was a solid enough architecture and a real MVP, not a finished plan. With AI, once-rigid choices are cheaper to reverse, so the better move is a simple prototype in front of real people, then iterate. Agonizing over the first library or chart rarely pays off as much as putting a week of shifts on someone’s phone.
