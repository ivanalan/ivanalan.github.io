## Outcome

1. Shipped the MVP in five months, on time, as a release for a European bank.
2. Unified a three-product workflow into one platform.
3. Established a longer-term vision for curated, persona-based navigation.

## One job, three products

Ab Initio's software collects, organizes, and transforms enterprise data at scale. My focus was data wrangling: turning raw data into something an analyst can actually report on.

Doing that took three separate products. You found raw data in Metadata>Portal, transformed and enriched it in Express>It, then set it to refresh on a schedule in Control>Center. Every handoff meant a new UI and a new mental model.

The pain points weren't subtle. The flow was fragmented, so context got lost at every switch. Each product was overwhelming, packed with features most users never touched. Plus, there was no formal design system, so there were three distinct user experiences.

## UX cost sales

Ab Initio relied on its technical prestige as its selling point, yet it had recently lost business purely on UX. This was new for Ab Initio, and it created appetite for a entirely new user experience. The team agreed on 3 key principles:

1. A single unified platform (and design system) instead of three.
2. Focus on user workflows instead of overwhelming users with all possible tools.
3. Curate experiences for different personas as not everyone has the same needs.

## Navigating ambiguity without data or research

Our organization was just starting to invest in UX and had no user data or research. So, I did what I could: I read documentation to understand the deeply technical landscape, met with other teams to understand the history of problems they had already solved, and had frequent 1:1s with my PM and engineering leads.

Then I wrote out the research questions I would have asked, hypotheses based on assumptions I made, and kept moving. Writing this down made the gap in evidence visible to everyone instead of always being a tension point in discussion.

## Collaborating to make the design vision a reality

My PM was an awesome collaborator and led the vision work. He described where the platform should be in three years, which products should fold into it, and why this work mattered.

Because UX was a new investment for our organization, many engineering managers had trouble seeing immediate value in this vision. That's where I came in: I crafted concrete flows that we could discuss and iterate on for the MVP deadline.

Interpolating between the design vision and our MVP implementation was my role for most of the five months. I'd take a direction from him, turn it into a more realistic flow with screens, get feedback for the MVP, then we'd both go back to the design vision and iterate.


## What I'd do differently

As a junior designer, I expressed every design decision through detailed user workflows. That started as a set of ten screens. After updating them at every weekly sync, I was maintaining fifty just to describe the architecture of the platform.

I should have started with a PRD, gotten every stakeholder to sign off on project objectives and user stories, and only then expressed them in design.
