## Outcome

1. Shipped an entirely new product in six months (on time) as a release for a European bank.
2. Unified a three-product workflow into one platform.
3. Established a longer-term vision for curated, persona-based navigation.

## One job, three products

Ab Initio's software collects, organizes, and transforms enterprise data at scale. Doing that took at least three separate products. To wrangle data, users found raw data in Metadata>Portal, transformed and enriched it in Express>It, then set it to refresh on a schedule in Control>Center.

Every handoff meant losing context and starting over with a new mental model. Each product was overwhelming, packed with features most users never touched. Plus, there was no formal design system, so there were three distinct user experiences.

## A deal lost on UX

Ab Initio had always sold on technical prestige, but it had just lost a deal purely on UX — proof that experience moves revenue, not just satisfaction. That gave us the support we needed from leadership to build a new product with user experience at the forefront.

The team aligned on three principles:

1. **Unified** — support the workflow in one platform instead of three products.
2. **Curated** — focus on user tasks instead of exposing every tool.
3. **Consistent** — one UI and, over time, one design system.

## Scoping the MVP

While there was a parallel effort to define a 2–3 year design vision, I focused on what we could ship in six months. Early reviews were less about pixels and more about forcing scope decisions:

- How curated should this MVP be versus a kitchen-sink port of legacy features?
- How much should we support legacy power users versus designing for new users of this platform?
- Which pieces of Metadata>Portal, Express>It, and Control>Center had to transfer for this workflow?

We landed on a wrangling-first MVP: find data, transform it, and schedule it — enough to replace the three-product handoff without boiling the ocean. Everything else stayed on the longer vision track.

## Navigating ambiguity without data or research

Our organization was just starting to invest in UX, so we had no user data or research. I read documentation to understand the technical landscape, met with other teams about problems they had already solved, and had frequent 1:1s with my PM and engineering leads.

Then I wrote out the research questions I would have asked, hypotheses based on assumptions I made, and kept moving. Writing this down made the gap in evidence visible to everyone instead of always being a tension point in discussion.

## Reuse vs. redesign

Unifying three products without a design system meant every pattern was a choice: copy what existed, or propose something better.

I first tried to reuse legacy patterns for consistency — for example, a bottom panel used elsewhere to inspect data. Reusing it in the new platform kept visual continuity, but it also propagated a weak experience. Consistency with a bad pattern wasn’t worth it.

For this product, I accepted short-term inconsistency and designed a side panel that better supported preview and context. That pattern could later land in the in-progress design system and roll out to other products.

## Collaborating to make the design vision a reality

My PM led the vision work: where the platform should be in three years, which products should fold into it, and why it mattered.

Because UX was a new investment, many engineering managers had trouble seeing immediate value in that vision. That’s where I came in: I turned direction into concrete flows we could debate against the MVP deadline. For most of the six months I interpolated between vision and shippable scope — take a direction, turn it into a realistic flow, get feedback for the MVP, then revisit the vision together.

Weekly reviews with a working group of ~15 helped surface technical constraints and conflicts early. When discussions drifted into implementation detail, I used timeboxed agendas and lower-fidelity artifacts so we stayed on the decision at hand.

## What I'd do differently

As a junior designer, I expressed every design decision through detailed user workflows. That started as a set of ten screens. After updating them at every weekly sync, I was maintaining fifty just to describe the architecture of the platform.

I should have started with a PRD, gotten stakeholders to sign off on objectives and user stories, and only then expressed them in design. The scoping questions above belonged in writing first — not only in wireframes.
