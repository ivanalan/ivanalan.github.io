## Outcome

1. Shipped the MVP in five months, on time, as a release for a European bank.
2. Collapsed a three-product workflow into one platform.
3. Established a longer-term vision to build toward: curated, persona-based navigation.

## One job, three products

Ab Initio's software collects, organizes, and transforms enterprise data at scale. My slice of it was data wrangling: turning raw data into something an analyst can actually report on.

Doing that took three separate products. You found raw data in Metadata>Portal, transformed and enriched it in Express>It, then set it to refresh on a schedule in Control>Center. Every handoff meant a new UI and a new mental model.

The pain points weren't subtle. The flow was fragmented, so context got lost at every switch. Each product was overwhelming, packed with features most users never touched; plus, there was no design system, so there were three distinct user experiences.

## The business case was already lost deals

Ab Initio is known for its technical prestige, and relied on that as its selling point; yet it had recently lost business purely on UX against competitors whose products had worse performance. This was a turning point, and it created appetite for a UX overhaul.

So the principles wrote themselves:

1. A single unified platform (and design system) instead of three.
2. Focus on user workflows; don't overwhelm users with all possible tools.
3. Curate experiences for different personas; not everyone has the same needs.

## I never talked to a single user

That's the honest constraint of this project. Enterprise data infrastructure at a bank is not a domain you get to go observe. Plus, our organization was just starting to invest in UX, let alone user interviews or research.

I did what I could instead: read documentation to navigate a deeply technical landscape, met with other teams to understand the history of problems they had already solved, and had frequent 1:1s with my PM and engineering leads.

Then I wrote out the primary research questions I would have asked, labeled them as hypothetical, and kept moving. Writing them down mattered more than I expected, as it made the gap in my evidence visible to everyone instead of quietly baked into my decisions.

## Collaborating to make the design vision a reality

My PM did the vision work, and he was fantastic at it. He could describe where the platform should be in three years, which products should fold into it, and sell to our entire organization why this mattered.

Because UX was a new investment for our organization, many engineering managers had trouble seeing immediate value in this vision. That's where I came in: I crafted concrete flows that we could discuss and iterate on for short-term work.

Interpolating between the design vision and our MVP implementation was my actual job for most of the five months. I'd take a direction from him, turn it into a flow with real screens and real states, get feedback for the MVP, and we would both iterate on the design vision again.

## Leading discussions away from screens and toward goals

My team consisted of fifteen developers and two dev leads, and for many of them this was their first exposure to UX at all.

It showed in how my work got reviewed. They read a mockup the way they'd read a spec, checking it for coverage, so early feedback arrived as a feature inventory: where's the option for this, why did you drop that setting, the copy on this is inaccurate. These are all valid points, but they surfaced at the wrong time in the SDLC.

I learned to speak to high-level user goals by presenting my work itself differently. Rather than presenting a workflow with frames, I would talk first through flow diagrams and information architecture, with supplemental low-fidelity visuals when needed.

## Ambiguity was the deliverable

The thing I got better at here wasn't visual design, it was documenting unknowns, assumptions, and hypotheses so the team could move fast without pretending we were certain.

The other half was designing with a vision in mind. The MVP had to launch, but the ideas that didn't fit couldn't just disappear, so I kept them in flight as a longer-term direction.

## What I'd do differently

As an organization new to product management, we had very few norms. Throughout my meetings with other teams, 1:1s, and my own team syncs, I often had to re-explain design decisions and hunt for notes. Keeping one organized doc would have been crucial to share with my many collaborators our team's future vision, the state of my work, how I got there, and why.
