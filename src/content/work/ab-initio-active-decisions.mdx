## From tracking metrics to automating decisions

## Sole designer with few resources

This was my first full-time UX role. I joined a team of 20 developers led by a senior engineer, with a vague mandate to lead Decision Flows from planning through implementation.

There were no user researchers, no direct users to consult, no use cases, no product manager, and no other designers on the team. The most important move I made was getting help: I set up weekly mentorship with a senior UX designer on another team and used those sessions for advice and critique.

## Turning a technical foundation into user-driven thinking

The only plan for how to get this work done was a 15+ page software architecture doc with no reference to the overall problem, personas, user flows, IA, UI, or timelines. Tasked with leading UX for this team, I took on the challenge of transforming this engineering-first implementation into a product that would meet real user needs.

I started by reverse-engineering user tasks from that doc and cross-checked them with proxy users — account managers. Then I led a workshop with our entire engineering team so we could work together and establish the main feature sets and their priorities. With this work, I was able to put together a UX north star to complement the technical architecture.

## Stepping into product management

With the UX north star built, I drafted an initial information architecture and was able to create more separate threads of work. With one year to launch, I stepped into product management and created a roadmap so that we could map dependencies and work efficiently.

## Stay low fidelity, ship the hard parts first

The project was large, the company was new to me, and the industry was unfamiliar, so I validated low-fidelity ideas early and often with my proxy users: account managers. With a tight deadline, I prioritized iterating in low fidelity on the most complex features and gave front-end engineers room to interpolate the rest.

The highest-impact work was flows: a way for users to author automated logic as flow charts. It would take the most effort, and was also critical to success, so I started early and ran recurring meetings to review high-level direction, interaction design, and technical constraints.

## Knowing when the MVP should increase scope

Feature creep was constant as there were no product managers; instead, technical leads ran both the software and product needs. At one point, our engineering lead (PM) pushed a testing mode where users could run an algorithm against historical data before production. But after consulting the account managers, it seemed like customers already had workable ways to test. This would have been a huge amount of work, jeopardizing the MVP, so I reframed this as a follow-up product-wide launch that would incorporate other monitoring features we had planned follow-ups for anyway.

Our navigation was different. Active>360 had a flat IA based on our runtime architecture that added a top-level page for every feature. Keeping that model for Decision Flows was the tipping point for this mental model, and was important enough to add to the MVP scope. I redesigned our entire navigation through stakeholder feedback so it could scale, resulting in navigation based on user workflows, not a technical architecture.

## What I’d do differently

Funding was cut before launch, so the early schedule and stakeholder appetite never became a shipped release. Still, the work changed how I operate: do more than “design screens,” even when that is the expectation. My work to turn this technical project into a user-driven product was valued by our team, even when it was an unexpected and new process.

However, I would also have protected low-fidelity exploration longer. Direction moved faster than it needed to, as I was worried about my roadmap, and a few more cheap iterations before locking flows would have helped.
