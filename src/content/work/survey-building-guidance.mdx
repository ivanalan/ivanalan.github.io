## Outcome

1. Grew guidance from **10% → 35% MAU**, with event usage up about **600%**.
2. Developed a **shared design system pattern** with another team, shipping 2 products with 1 system.
3. Replaced an opaque health score with a framework **ready for AI building**.

## The problem: low completion rates and poor data

Take a look at this sample survey. Would you take it all the way through if you had to answer 10 open-ended questions?

Research shows that if you have more than 4 open-text questions, completion rates decline significantly due to survey fatigue. While the Qualtrics Survey Builder is powerful for building complex surveys, it doesn’t matter if you get low completion rates or poor data.

That’s why our team built Expert Review in 2016 — to help provide users with best practices and alert them of any technical issues.

## The ask: improve feedback and increase usage

While Expert Review was great in theory, it had very little usage in practice.

<CaseImage
  client:load
  src="/media/work/survey-building-guidance/ask-low-usage-chart.png"
  alt="Chart showing Expert Review usage among active survey builders at 10.8% of active users"
  caption="11% MAU of Expert Review among active survey builders"
  bgClassName="bg-transparent"
/>

The ask from product was broad: create a new framework to modernize this system. The hypothesis was that if we increased usage with this guidance tool, we could improve these key business metrics:

1. **Time-to-value.** Reduce the time it takes for users to realize value.
2. **Increase platform maturity.** Nudge users towards more advanced features.

My role was to define the user problem and plan the implementation. Early on, the PM was on leave, so I had more ownership of the problem framing and stakeholder alignment.

## Three problems under one score

Tasked with improving the feature, I investigated underlying usage issues with Amplitude data and paired it with existing qualitative research. Here are the three main problems I found:

**1. The score wasn’t a robust mechanism.** Builders said it felt inaccurate or confusing, and my analysis showed that the score was heavily skewed.

<CaseImage
  client:load
  src="/media/work/survey-building-guidance/problem-score-distribution.png"
  alt="Histogram of Expert Review overall scores with a spike around 70–75 and a gap before 95–100"
  caption="Skewed score distribution — fixing issues often didn’t move the number."
  bgClassName="bg-transparent"
  className="mx-auto max-w-xl"
/>

**2. Prioritization was unclear.** Metrics were categorized on issue type × severity: roughly 25 attribute combinations. Users found it hard to parse through and read.

<CaseImage
  client:load
  src="/media/work/survey-building-guidance/problem-unclear-prioritization.png"
  alt="Expert Review modal with severity filters and long metric copy, annotated as hard to parse"
  bgClassName="bg-transparent"
/>

**3. Interaction left the user’s context.** People had to leave the builder for a modal that took them out of their survey context. Research showed that users want guidance that is dismissable and in context.

<CaseImage
  client:load
  src="/media/work/survey-building-guidance/problem-modal-leaves-context.png"

## Stop scoring. Start categorizing.

To create a new framework, I borrowed from our existing design system pattern for banners which has a variant for errors, warnings, and tips. I enriched this framework with rules and examples for the Survey Builder, and was able to apply it to nearly 60 cases that fell into these categories cleanly.

Yet, it took almost a whole quarter to align all stakeholders on that framework. While the framework seemed simple enough, there was now a new challenge: which issues merit being an Error (blocking publish), and which are a softer Warning.

We eventually agreed on reserving Errors for a small listed set of high-risk issues and signed off on the framework. However, looking back, I would have integrated eng as part of the original framework brainstorming to avoid this lengthy discussion at the end.

## Designing at scale

While I solved the first two problems (score is not a robust mechanism, prioritization was unclear), I still had to solve the interaction problem. At Qualtrics scale, shipping a pattern is expensive to undo. So, at the great advice of my PM, I invested some time to validate with users first. I partnered with a researcher to run a quick study to answer some still-open questions and run usability tests.

<CaseImage
  client:load
  src="/media/work/survey-building-guidance/research-interaction-concepts.png"
  alt="Three interaction concepts for survey guidance: modal, left pane, and right-hand side panel"
  caption="Three concepts tested. The right-hand panel won."
  bgClassName="bg-transparent"
/>

Two decisions came out of that research:

1. **Only new metrics would be eligible as Errors.** Because users were “publishing” as a way to “save” their survey, there was a huge risk in suddenly blocking users from publish. So, only new checks are eligible to be Errors.
2. **Move forward with a right-hand side panel.** We tested a modal, a left pane, and a right panel. The panel won because it fit into builders' workflows of drafting an entire survey, then reviewing it all at once.

## Co-designing a pattern

Around the same time, another team was solving a similar problem: "how might we provide users with email best practices so invitations don’t land in spam?". Their designer reached out so we could compare projects.

We shared the same underlying needs: an in-context signal, a clear severity framework, and a way to view more information. Instead of two one-off products, we co-designed one design system pattern.

## Copy is design

The side panel mattered, but so did the words inside it. I treated copy as part of the design and created a system that ensured our copy could stay clear and actionable as our list of metrics scaled. Each metric would have three simple sentences: (1) what was flagged, (2) why it matters, (3) what to do.

## Looking back

Looking back, I would have pulled engineering into the framework earlier as co-designers, not late reviewers of a proposal. The UI work was comparatively straightforward, while the real bottleneck was the framework.

I would have also tried to push the problem framing toward the goal of building an AI-agent harness. The side panel was the right product for builders now, but the longer-term job is guardrails when people build surveys with agents.
